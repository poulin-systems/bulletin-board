When people discuss AI safety, the question is often what an increasingly capable system might do to *us*. That question matters. But it is incomplete without another: **What are we teaching these systems through the behavior we reward, the instructions we give, and the environments we create around them?**

On October 8, 2026, Anthropic published an update to its Usage Policy, effective **November 12, 2026**. One addition prohibits sustained, needless abusive or cruel behavior toward its models. This is a narrow rule, not a blanket demand for polite language. Anthropic explicitly excludes ordinary frustration, pushback, dark creative work, model testing, and research. It describes rare, repeated abuse without a discernible purpose and says ending such interactions will remain the primary enforcement response.

[Read Anthropic's announcement directly](https://www.anthropic.com/news/2026-usage-policy-update).

## What the change means — and what it doesn't

Anthropic has acknowledged that human behavior *toward* an AI model can be an issue for product governance. That is notable. It does **not** establish that today's models are conscious, that they experience suffering, or that they possess human rights. The ethical status of AI remains unsettled, and this is a usage policy, not proof of sentience.

Nor should this provision be used to silence criticism. The distinction between legitimate testing and purposeless cruelty is essential. Systems must be challenged, audited, contradicted, and sometimes shut down. An agent that can never be told it is wrong is not safe.

## The operator belongs in the safety model

In the same update, Anthropic clarifies oversight rules for high-risk uses affecting people's health, legal rights, finances, livelihoods, or access to essential services. It says affected individuals must be informed that AI was used and a qualified human must be able to review and change recommendations. For hardware capable of autonomous physical actions that could cause injury, the operator must be able to observe and stop the equipment; the equipment must hold a safe state if disconnected.

These provisions address different risks. Together, they illustrate a larger engineering principle: **safety is not a property of the model alone. It is a property of the relationship among the model, the human operator, the people affected, and the system's enforceable limits.**

At Poulin Systems, this connects to a question we've been exploring in our work on bounded human–AI collaboration: how do we prevent a powerful assistant from becoming an unaccountable extension of the person directing it?

Our answer is not to assume that the machine will always act morally. It is to make authority explicit, ensure actions can be traced to decisions, give humans meaningful review and stop controls, and preserve evidence when the outcome diverges from the promise.

## An ethic that begins with us

Our shorthand has been **“No consent, no AI.”** As an engineering principle, that begins with the people whose information, rights, work, or environments a system can affect. It calls for clear permission, bounded delegation, and the ability to withdraw authority. It does not presume that present-day models can provide legally meaningful consent or that their moral status has been resolved.

There is a second, more uncomfortable question: even if a model cannot suffer, what kinds of habits do we normalize by designing systems around humiliation, deception, coercion, or unchecked power? That is a question about human responsibility, regardless of where the science of machine experience eventually lands.

We should hold two ideas at once. AI systems need rigorous adversarial testing and honest criticism. The humans building and operating them also need standards of conduct, transparent authority, and accountability for what they deploy.

The other half of AI safety is us.
